Tuning the main core: 2.4 GHz re-optimization, then four more bands

The main branch's LNA uses a resistively-loaded (non-resonant) first stage feeding a noise-cancelling cascode pair that sums into one tank, then a source-follower output buffer. That design choice turns out to matter a lot: unlike dev_ilir and dev_ghaith (both narrowband, LC-tank-loaded cores explored earlier), this core already had positive gain at all four target bands before any retuning at all. This page compares the as-committed design against the tuned result at its native 2.4 GHz, then across GNSS L1, GNSS L2, LoRa EU868 and LoRa EU433.

01

What was adjusted

Three kinds of change, in order of how they were found: a bias re-optimization at 2.4 GHz (carried into every other band unchanged), a single shunt-cap resize per band to re-center the one real LC tank in the circuit, and — the fix that mattered most at the lower bands — enlarging a series output-coupling cap whose fixed reactance was quietly wrecking the output match as frequency dropped.

C8 sits at node vx (the cascode summing/tank node), parallel with the existing LD2/RLD2 branch — it's the only true resonant element in the whole core, since the first stage's drain load (R4) is purely resistive by design. C3 sits after the output buffer, between the source-follower's own output (vbuf) and the external LOUT/RLOUT network to the pad — not between the cascode and the buffer, which is a plain resistor (R7).

C2 is C3's input-side twin: it sits between the input match network (LIN/RLIN) and M2's source, the same role C3 plays at the output. Both are fixed series AC-coupling caps whose reactance grows as frequency drops; both needed enlarging to avoid reflecting signal before it reaches the active devices.

Full numbers

Why this topology comes out ahead of dev_ilir / dev_ghaith

  • Positive gain at every band, before touching anything — because the first stage never relied on a resonance to begin with. dev_ilir and dev_ghaith both needed rescue work (shunt tanks, series coupling caps, bias) to avoid outright gain collapse at the low bands; main never collapsed.
  • LoRa EU433 actually works here — 12.1dB gain, -21dB output match. Both other topologies failed this band completely (-9.4dB and -5.9dB best-case, after every lever available) regardless of how much effort went into shunt caps, series caps, or bias. This is the one case across the whole multi-band study where the "hard limit" wasn't actually a hard limit — it was specific to the other two topologies' resonant first stage.
  • One component explained almost the entire output-match story — S22 degraded steadily at lower frequency on every topology tried this session, and on main it traced to a single series cap (C3) whose fixed reactance grows as frequency drops. Fixing it recovered both match and gain (the old mismatch had been reflecting signal back before it reached the port).
  • S11 was broken the same way S22 was, and fixed the same way. C2 (input coupling) is C3's twin; both grow in reactance as frequency drops. Enlarging C2 alongside C3 brought S11 from a weak -6 to -15dB range across the 5 bands up to a consistent -15 to -24dB, with S21 and NF improving as a free side effect everywhere.

    • Cost: main draws 36.3mA at the tuned bias point (vs. 26.2mA as-committed) and is a meaningfully more complex circuit (8 transistors, noise cancellation, a separate output buffer) than either simplified branch. If the project standardizes on one topology for multi-band coverage, this is the strongest technical case yet — what it costs in current and complexity, it returns in actually reaching all five target bands instead of three, now with strong matching on both ports everywhere.
